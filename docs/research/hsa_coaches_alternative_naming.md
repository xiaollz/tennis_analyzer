# HSA Across Coaching Systems: One Mechanism, Many Names

**Research focus.** Horizontal Shoulder Adduction (HSA) — closing the angle between the pectoral and the humerus, driven primarily by pectoralis major with anterior deltoid as a synergist — is one of the largest single contributors to forward racquet velocity in the modern forehand. Most coaches teach the *outcome* of HSA (the racquet ending up across the body, the hand traveling toward the opposite ear, the cross-body finish, the windshield wiper, the lasso) but very few explicitly name the underlying mechanism. This document maps the alternative naming, identifies which systems make the mechanism explicit, and documents why HSA tends to be the "invisible" power source.

Date: 2026-05-03. Compiled from public web sources, FTT articles, Brian Gordon's TennisPlayer.net work, and existing local research at `/Users/qsy/Desktop/tennis/docs/research/`.

## 2. Coach-by-Coach Coverage

### 2.1 Brian Gordon / TennisSpeed Research / TennisPlayer.net

Gordon is the only public source backing his claims with 3D motion-capture data on ATP players. His "Four Pillars of the ATP Type III" framework explicitly separates the contributions of horizontal adduction, internal shoulder rotation, and elbow extension. On a TennisPlayer.net forum he wrote that one can achieve full racquet-head-velocity modulation "solely with horizontal adduction" without torso rotation, and that "elbow extension" carries leverage on par with internal rotation.

Gordon's published numbers (often cited as "ISR ≈ 50% of RHS") attribute the remainder primarily to torso rotation plus horizontal adduction/flexion. Local synthesis at `/Users/qsy/Desktop/tennis/docs/research/brian_gordon_video_analyses/SUMMARY.md` records this as the anchor for the entire knowledge base — Gordon's data resolves disagreements between FTT, RTP, TPA, and the others.

What Gordon does *not* do: package HSA as a felt cue. He measures it. The translation from "the data says pec major fires 50–100 ms before contact" to "press your chest forward to send your hand into the ball" is left to coaches.

### 2.2 FTT / Hugh Clarke — the only system that names HSA as the engine

FTT is unique in the surveyed corpus because it labels the muscle, the timing, and the cue. Three articles are load-bearing:

**Press Slot.** "A dynamic position you reach by pressing with your pecs." HSA is described as "the angle between the humerus and the chest closes." Timing reference: about 50–100 ms before contact, the trunk slows and pec tension fires the elbow into horizontal adduction. (https://faulttoleranttennis.com/the-forehand-press-slot/)

**Forehand POWER Checklist.** "The chest contracts to force the arm out in front of the body." It also explicitly cautions against pec stretch-reflex use in casual play — "Using a stretch reflex at the chest is, generally, not worth it" — because the timing is brittle. (https://faulttoleranttennis.com/forehand-power-checklist/)

**The Most Critical Part of the Forehand (Horizontal Shoulder Adduction).** The dedicated YouTube lecture (id `5KdScDKxVSI`). The local analysis at `/Users/qsy/Desktop/tennis/docs/research/ftt_video_analyses/5KdScDKxVSI.md` records that this video frames HSA as the core forward-velocity engine, ties HSA tolerance to body-height adjustment, and flags Nadal's lasso finish as a *consequence* of constrained HSA range. Direct user-internalized point: "胸部挤压" (chest squeeze) is the felt cue.

**Stop Finishing Over Your Shoulder.** Argues that the finish is downstream and aesthetically misleading. The fetched content does not frame the article in HSA terms specifically; it focuses on prioritizing strike intention over follow-through shape. (https://faulttoleranttennis.com/stop-finishing-over-your-shoulder/)

FTT therefore is the public coaching source most aligned with the user's thesis — it names HSA, links it to the pec, and pins down the timing window.

### 2.3 Patrick Mouratoglou Academy

Mouratoglou's published coaching corner discusses the "offset" forehand and pictures the racquet finishing high near the opposite shoulder, but the available pages frame this as torso rotation plus left-shoulder retraction, not as pec-driven horizontal adduction. The academy's video lessons ("How to unlock the full potential of your forehand," "Forehand Mechanics") use cues like "shoulder pushing forward" and "racquet crossing" — outcome language.

Verdict: outcome teaching, no explicit HSA mechanism in the surveyed material.

Source: https://www.mouratoglou.com/en/conseils-coaching/coaching-corner/tennis-technique/forehand-techniques-and-tips/

## 6. Why HSA Is Underemphasized

Six structural reasons emerge from the sources:

1. **It is fast.** Peak HSA contribution occurs in roughly the last 50–100 ms before contact (FTT timing reference). Slow-motion video at 60 fps captures roughly six frames in that window; 240 fps captures twenty-four. At normal speed it is invisible.
2. **It is masked by torso rotation.** The torso and the upper arm rotate in approximately the same plane on a forehand. To the eye, "torso rotates and arm comes around" looks like one motion. Decoupling requires either 3D motion-capture (Gordon's tool) or the FTT thought experiment of "lock the torso, just press the chest" (the locked-stance HSA isolation drill).
3. **It is confused with pronation.** Pronation occurs at the radio-ulnar joint (forearm), not at the gleno-humeral joint. When players say "pronation," they often mean the combination of HSA + ISR + actual pronation. Lewit's "lasso physics" article exemplifies this: the ISR + pronation pair becomes the explanation, and HSA disappears.
4. **It happens internally.** Unlike a leg drive (you can see the heel push) or a torso rotation (you can see the back foot kick out), HSA happens between two body segments that are both already rotating. There is no big visible change, only a closing of an angle.
5. **It is mistakenly assumed to be the "follow-through."** Several articles surveyed (the Tennis Bros buggy-whip page, PlayYourCourt, Mouratoglou) treat the across-body shape as follow-through styling, not as the strike-phase muscle action that produces it. The FTT "Stop Finishing Over Your Shoulder" article inverts this: the finish is downstream; the strike is upstream.
6. **The vocabulary is anatomical, not athletic.** "Horizontal shoulder adduction" is a cadaveric term. Coaches teach in feel, intention, and metaphor — windshield wiper, lasso, slingshot. Translating from the anatomy term to a usable cue requires a coach willing to do that bridging work; few are.

## 7. Counter-Evidence

Honest accounting requires capturing positions that argue against an HSA-centric model:

- **Torso-rotation primacy.** Several systems (PlayYourCourt; Will Hamilton's "Kinetic Forehand"; Online Tennis Instruction) argue that core/hip rotation is the primary engine and the arm is essentially a transmission — "a rope to transfer force." Under this view HSA is real but minor relative to torso rotation.
- **Stretch-reflex skepticism.** FTT itself says "Using a stretch reflex at the chest is, generally, not worth it" — i.e., even the system that names HSA cautions against trying to weaponize it via aggressive pec stretch, because the timing is brittle.
- **ISR-primacy.** Brian Gordon's "ISR ≈ 50% of RHS" framing places internal shoulder rotation, not horizontal adduction, as the largest single contributor on a Type-III ATP forehand. HSA is a major contributor but not necessarily the largest in his data; the published dominance of HSA at 45–48% is associated with Sasaki-style decompositions where the racquet's *forward* (horizontal) velocity is isolated separately from the *upward* / spin-producing velocity.
- **Synergy framing.** Multiple academic sources (e.g. PMC11535041 on serve-receiving forehands, MDPI sensors paper on cross-court vs inside-out forehands) treat HSA as one of several joint contributions whose ranking shifts by shot type. There is no single "HSA contributes X%" number that holds across all forehands.

The defensible synthesis: HSA is one of the two or three largest contributors to the racquet's velocity vector at contact on a typical Type-III forehand, the contribution is largest in the forward (toward-net) direction, and ISR is the largest contributor in the upward / spin direction. Coaches who collapse this into a single "pull across" cue lose the directional decomposition; coaches who go pure-torso lose HSA entirely.

## 8. Implication for the Personal Knowledge Base

The user's existing system already has the muscle named:

- `project_scapular_slot_bible.md` (4/30): scapular slot enables the upper-arm to be "carried" by the trunk, which is the loading half of the HSA window.
- `project_pec_elbow_drive.md` (4/30): pec-pushes-elbow is the released-half label for HSA.
- `project_isr_sinner_model.md` (5/3): ISR fires *after* HSA peaks, and is itself the consequence of pec release. This is consistent with FTT's "press slot" timing.

The HSA "naming gap" identified in this report is therefore not a gap in the user's personal model — it is a gap in the public coaching market. The user's training timeline (3/15 → 5/3) effectively reinvented FTT's HSA framework via felt experience plus Brian Gordon's data anchor. The other ten coaching systems surveyed would not have led the user to the same internalization on the same timeline, because their cue vocabularies do not point at the muscle.

Two practical actions follow:

1. When watching new coach videos, run a 30-second test: does the coach name pec / horizontal adduction / press, or only the cross-body finish? If only the finish, downgrade the video as "outcome teaching" and keep it for visual reference, not for mechanism.
2. When self-diagnosing, do not trust the visual finish. The lasso, the windshield wiper, the across-body — all are downstream. The diagnostic question is "did the chest fire 50–100 ms before contact?" Local cue: 胸部挤压 / chest squeeze / press slot.

## 10. Limits of This Survey

- TalkTennis (`tt.tennis-warehouse.com`) was unreachable during the research session due to a TLS certificate verification failure. Direct quote-mining of analytical posters (e.g. Brian Gordon-aligned threads) was therefore limited to second-hand citations.
- TennisPlayer.net members-only articles (parts of Gordon's "Developing an ATP Forehand" series, the windshield-wiper sample article URL) returned HTTP 403 to the unauthenticated fetcher. The public Four-Pillars page was used as the primary Gordon source.
- Reddit's site-restricted searches returned no high-signal HSA threads. Reddit forehand-power language remains primarily about lag, legs, and looseness rather than pec major.
- Sasaki 2022's specific 45–48% figure for HSA contribution to forward racquet velocity could not be directly confirmed in the available search corpus during this session — adjacent biomechanics papers were located, but the specific Sasaki citation may live behind a journal paywall. The user-supplied figure is preserved as-is in the framing, with the caveat that the exact decomposition depends on the paper's velocity-vector definition.
- Non-English coach systems (Spanish academies, Croatian/Eastern European schools beyond Aracic, Australian schools beyond Tennis Bros) were not deeply mined.

These limits do not change the headline conclusion: of public-facing English-language coaching content, FTT and Brian Gordon are the two systems that explicitly name HSA as the engine; the remainder teach the visible outcome.
