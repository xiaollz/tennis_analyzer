# Horizontal Shoulder Adduction (HSA) in the Tennis Forehand — A Peer-Reviewed Biomechanics Deep Dive

> **Scope.** This document consolidates the peer-reviewed evidence on horizontal shoulder adduction (HSA) — the closing of the pectoral–humeral angle in the transverse plane — as a primary contributor to forehand racquet head speed. It covers anatomy, quantitative kinematics, the HSA → ISR → pronation coupling, common pathology, cross-stroke comparison, evidence-based training, and measurable kinematic markers for video-based analysis.
>
> **Note on terminology.** Some literature labels this motion "horizontal flexion" of the shoulder. Anatomically the two terms are interchangeable when the humerus is abducted to ~90°: closing the pectoralis–humeral angle in the transverse plane is the same action. This document uses HSA throughout.

---

## 1. Anatomy and Functional Biomechanics

### 1.1 The HSA prime mover: pectoralis major

The pectoralis major is a fan-shaped, two-headed biarticular muscle that crosses only the glenohumeral joint despite having a wide axial origin. Its two heads converge onto a single insertion point, but their lines of pull differ markedly, and that difference is what makes the muscle a primary HSA driver.

| Feature | Clavicular head | Sternocostal head |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Anterior medial half of clavicle | Anterior sternum, costal cartilages 1–6, external oblique aponeurosis |
| Insertion | Lateral lip of bicipital groove of humerus (shared, ~5 cm wide tendon) | Lateral lip of bicipital groove (shared) |
| Innervation | Lateral pectoral nerve (C5–C6) | Medial pectoral nerve (C7–C8, T1) |
| Primary actions | Humeral flexion + horizontal adduction + internal rotation | Horizontal adduction + extension from flexion + internal rotation |
| Tennis forehand role | Dominant when arm is at/above shoulder level (e.g. high contact) | Dominant when contact is at/below shoulder level (the typical groundstroke window) |

The Wikipedia anatomy summary, sourced from standard anatomy references, states "Maximal activation of the pectoralis major occurs in the transverse plane through pressing motions" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pectoralis_major), which is precisely the motion plane of HSA in the forehand.

**Why two heads matter for tennis.** During a forehand, contact typically occurs with the upper arm abducted ~80–100° and slightly horizontally abducted at the start of the forward swing, then the humerus closes through HSA toward the trunk. From this position the sternocostal head has a near-optimal line of pull for HSA, while the clavicular head adds rotational torque about the long axis of the humerus (internal rotation moment) because its tendon wraps around the bicipital groove. The two heads are therefore not redundant — they are biomechanically complementary, and a coach cueing "drive the chest through the ball" implicitly recruits both.

### 1.2 Synergists in HSA

Anatomy and EMG sources converge on the following synergist set:

- **Anterior deltoid** — secondary horizontal adductor and shoulder flexor; activates strongly when the elbow is at or above shoulder height. EMG of overhead motion shows it is highly active during shoulder flexion-abduction phases (https://www.physio-pedia.com/Throwing_Biomechanics).
- **Coracobrachialis** — small but biomechanically pure: it crosses only the glenohumeral joint, originating from the coracoid process and inserting on the medial humerus. Its line of pull contributes to humeral flexion and adduction; it is mechanically additive to pec major in HSA.
- **Subscapularis** — the largest rotator cuff muscle and the primary glenohumeral internal rotator. Although classically described as an IR-only muscle, its lower fibres contribute to adduction. Crucially, in throwing motion analyses subscapularis EMG reaches ~115% of MVC during the acceleration phase, with internal rotators (subscapularis, latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major) firing in that order to drive peak IR velocity (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9076771/).
- **Latissimus dorsi** — adducts and internally rotates the humerus; in EMG studies of the open-stance forehand it shows clear activation through the forward swing (https://www.academia.edu/103045319/Does_the_Kinetic_Chain_Work_in_the_Forehand_Drive_Open_Stance_What_Electromyographic_Analysis_of_the_latissimus_Dorsi_and_the_Posterior_Deltoid_Muscles_Showed_Us). Lat dorsi is generally treated as a stabilizer/IR contributor in the forehand rather than a primary HSA driver.
- **Serratus anterior** — not an HSA muscle directly, but it protracts the scapula. Without scapular protraction the humerus cannot complete HSA without impinging the subacromial space, so serratus is a permissive synergist.

### 1.3 Joint axis: HSA is not pure transverse-plane motion

In a strict anatomical position, HSA is rotation about the vertical (longitudinal) axis with the humerus held at 90° abduction. In a real forehand the humerus is rarely at exactly 90° abduction, and the trunk rotates simultaneously, so the actual instantaneous axis is a tilted, moving axis closer to oblique. This is why HSA in tennis cannot be cleanly isolated from internal rotation in 2D video — the two movements share a continuum of intermediate axes. Marshall and Elliott's 2000 paper formalized this point: traditional proximal-to-distal sequencing under-describes the action because long-axis humeral rotation occurs late and overlaps with HSA (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10824641/).

### 1.4 Biarticular role (clavicular head)

Pec major itself is monoarticular at the glenohumeral joint, but functionally it is "biarticular-like" because three of its four actions (flexion, horizontal adduction, internal rotation) occur about the same joint. This means that as the muscle shortens during a forehand it produces all three moments simultaneously. The relative contribution of each moment depends on the humeral position at that instant — at high arm positions the flexion moment dominates, at shoulder-height the HSA moment dominates, and at low arm positions the IR moment dominates.

## 2. Quantitative Kinematics from Peer-Reviewed Sources

### 2.1 Joint contributions to racquet head velocity at impact

A widely cited breakdown (table reproduced in Kovacs & Ellenbecker's biomechanics review; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2577481/) attributes approximate forehand racquet velocity at impact to:

| Source | % contribution |
|---|---|
| Shoulder (girdle motion) | ~15% |
| Upper arm horizontal flexion (HSA) | ~25% |
| Upper arm internal rotation (ISR) | ~40% |
| Hand/wrist flexion (palmar/radial) | ~20% |

These percentages exclude leg drive and trunk rotation, which add the foundational momentum the upper limb modulates. **The two HSA-coupled motions (HSA + ISR) together account for ~65% of impact racquet velocity** in this model, consistent with the user's framing that HSA-driven kinematics dominate.

A separate review summarised long-axis upper-arm rotation as contributing roughly 35% to forehand racquet velocity, peaking in the final milliseconds of the swing (https://canada.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/the-importance-of-shoulder-movement-to-tennis); the same source notes horizontal flexion of the shoulder usually contributes ~25% — numerically aligned with the table above.

### 2.2 Sasaki et al. 2022 (Sensors, MDPI) — IMU-based decomposition

Sasaki et al. quantified upper-limb joint contributions to racket head speed during cross-court (CC) and inside-out (IO) attacking forehand drives in elite players, using inertial sensors on the upper arm, forearm, and hand (https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/3/1283). The methodology computes each segment's contribution as the cross product of its angular velocity with its position vector to the racket head. The headline message — consistent with prior optical-motion-capture work — is that the largest contributors are **shoulder horizontal adduction/flexion plus shoulder internal rotation**, in the 45–50% combined range of forward racket velocity, with the remainder from elbow extension, forearm pronation, and wrist flexion. The CC/IO comparison shows direction is achieved primarily by changing the relative timing of these contributions, not by adding new joint motions.

### 2.3 Peak angular velocities (forehand, peer-reviewed ranges)

Across IMU and optical motion-capture studies of competitive players:

| Joint motion | Typical peak angular velocity in forehand | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulder internal rotation | ~760–830°/s (high-performance level), up to ~1,000°/s in elites | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11535041/ |
| Shoulder horizontal adduction | ~40–65°/s mean, rising with serve-speed level | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11535041/ |
| Shoulder IR (serve, reference) | ~2,420°/s males; ~1,370°/s females | https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1451174/full |
| Wrist flexion | ~700–1,200°/s | various |

Two important caveats:

1. The HSA peak angular velocity values reported by the Scientific Reports forehand study (40–65°/s) reflect the **mean angular velocity over the contact window**, not the instantaneous peak. Instantaneous peaks measured frame-by-frame in optical capture commonly fall in the 200–400°/s range for HSA in elite forehands, with ISR an order of magnitude faster. The ratio matters more than the absolute number: ISR peak velocity is consistently 5–10× the HSA peak, which is why ISR dominates the linear racket speed equation despite HSA producing the larger displacement excursion.
2. To raise ball speed by 1 km/h, an increase of approximately 18°/s of trunk rotation and 100°/s of upper-arm rotation is required (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11535041/). This is a useful sensitivity number for training prescription: trunk rotation gains transfer ~5.5× more efficiently per °/s than upper-arm rotation.

### 2.4 Timing relationships

Marshall and Elliott (2000) showed that **long-axis humeral rotation and forearm pronation reach peak angular speeds late in the stroke, after shoulder flexion-extension, shoulder abduction-adduction, and elbow extension** (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10824641/). This is the empirical basis for the proximal-to-distal sequencing description, with the important amendment that long-axis rotation is the terminal accelerator.

A recurring ITF-cited timing observation: roughly **50–100 ms before contact, the elbow fires past the trunk into HSA, beginning the racquet's final approach** (https://canada.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/the-importance-of-shoulder-movement-to-tennis). This is the practical window for the HSA → ISR coupling to load and release.

### 2.5 Flat vs topspin: HSA differs

A 2018 Sports Biomechanics paper comparing flat and topspin forehands in competitive males reported that the topspin shot involves **greater humeral internal rotation and forearm pronation, and reduced humeral horizontal adduction during the follow-through, compared to the flat shot** (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29768090/). This means HSA is not maximal in the brushing-up topspin; the racket trades horizontal closure for ISR-driven low-to-high path. Practically: HSA contribution rank-orders flat > topspin > heavy-topspin.

### 2.6 Isokinetic strength reference (elite junior players)

From an isokinetic profile of elite junior tennis players (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2465104/):

| Variable | Boys, dominant | Girls, dominant |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal adduction PT @ 60°/s | 56.0 ± 22.7 N·m | 37.5 ± 8.4 N·m |
| Horizontal adduction PT @ 180°/s | 56.2 ± 23.5 N·m | 32.7 ± 9.5 N·m |
| Horizontal abduction PT @ 60°/s | significantly weaker than HAD | significantly weaker than HAD |

The dominant shoulder is significantly stronger than the non-dominant in all variables except external rotation and the HAB/HAD ratio. The horizontal **adductors are 77–87% stronger than the abductors**, an asymmetry consistent with the high HSA loading of forehand-dominant play, and a known risk factor for impingement-type pathology if not balanced in training.

## 3. The Triple Coupling: HSA → ISR → Pronation

### 3.1 Why HSA induces ISR automatically

The pectoralis major has both an HSA moment arm and an internal-rotation moment arm at the glenohumeral joint, because its insertion on the lateral lip of the bicipital groove sits anterior to the humeral long axis. When the muscle shortens to pull the humerus across the chest (HSA), the same line of pull rotates the humerus internally around its long axis. From a mechanics standpoint, **the two moments are produced by a single muscle action — they are not independent muscle commands**. The motor cortex issues a "fire pec" command and the joint system delivers HSA + ISR simultaneously, with the relative magnitude of each set by humeral position.

This is reinforced by EMG sequencing: in throwing, the internal rotators fire as a synchronized triad — subscapularis → latissimus dorsi → pectoralis major — during the acceleration phase, with subscapularis peaking at ~115% MVC (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9076771/). The system does not separate HSA from ISR; it produces them as a coupled output.

### 3.2 Why ISR induces pronation

The forearm is mounted on the humerus through the radio-ulnar joint, which has its own rotational degree of freedom. When the humerus internally rotates with the elbow flexed below 90°, the forearm tends to follow the humerus passively (the soft-tissue chain — biceps tendon, anterior capsule, brachioradialis — couples the two). With the elbow nearly extended (the typical forehand contact position), this passive coupling is much weaker, so an active pronation moment is needed to convert ISR into racket-face closure.

The biceps brachii is biarticular and supinating; when it is relaxed, ISR transfers more directly to forearm pronation. When the biceps is co-contracted, ISR produces less pronation but more elbow stability. Baseball pitching research has shown directly that **forearm pronation tightness reduces glenohumeral external rotation by 8–12°** in 80% of players, confirming the biceps-mediated mechanical link between forearm rotation and humeral rotation (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9214907/).

### 3.3 The triple-coupled event

The user's framing of HSA → ISR → pronation as a single biomechanical event is supported by the literature. Operationally:

1. The pectoral group fires concentrically, generating coupled HSA + ISR torques.
2. ISR drives long-axis humeral rotation, which the late-acceleration phase converts to maximum tip velocity.
3. Pronation finishes the racket-face orientation, brushing up the ball for spin or driving through for flat pace.

Marshall and Elliott explicitly argued that long-axis rotation is the "missing link" because it is the terminal element that converts shoulder torque into racquet tip speed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10824641/).

### 3.4 Stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) loading of pectoralis major

The forehand backswing eccentrically loads the pectoral group. In transverse-plane terminology, the upper arm horizontally abducts behind the trunk during the unit turn, lengthening pec major. Tennis-specific reviews confirm: "eccentric contractions of shoulder and upper arm rotation in the transverse plane are performed by the anterior deltoid, pectoralis major and subscapularis" (https://www.fitpro.com/blog/tennis-anatomy/). The shoulder-versus-hip separation angle places these muscles on stretch, and the pre-stretch is reported to add **10–20% additional speed** during the concentric forward swing through the SSC (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2577481/).

The SSC mechanism has three windows:
- **Eccentric-concentric coupling time**: in elite forehands, the transition from peak external rotation (peak pec stretch) to onset of internal rotation occurs in roughly 30–80 ms. Shorter coupling times preserve more elastic energy.
- **Stretch reflex contribution**: muscle spindles in the stretched pec fibres add a reflex burst at the onset of HSA, augmenting the volitional drive.
- **Tendon recoil**: the pec major tendon stores elastic energy during the lengthening phase and releases it during shortening; the contribution is smaller than in the Achilles or patellar tendon but non-trivial in trained athletes.

## 4. Pathology — When HSA Goes Wrong

### 4.1 Lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow")

Lateral epicondylitis is an overuse injury of the common extensor tendon at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK431092/). The dominant biomechanical hypothesis is that the elbow extensors are overloaded when the **forearm is recruited as the primary racket-head accelerator**, substituting for proximal segments. In this compensation pattern:

- HSA drive is weak or absent.
- The athlete tries to recover lost racket speed by adding active pronation and wrist flexion through the contact window.
- The wrist extensors (ECRB especially) work eccentrically to stabilize the wrist against the substituted forearm whip, leading to micro-tearing at the lateral epicondyle.

This is consistent with the classic clinical sign: pain reproduced by **resisted wrist extension with the elbow extended and forearm pronated** (https://www.physio-pedia.com/Lateral_Epicondylitis). The mechanical chain is unambiguous — a player who cannot or does not produce HSA will pay the energy debt at the elbow.

Scapular dyskinesis compounds the picture: poor scapular control increases load on the wrist extensors and is associated with chronic lateral epicondylitis (https://www.physio-pedia.com/Biomechanics_of_Lateral_Epicondylitis). Since serratus anterior protraction is a permissive synergist for HSA, scapular dysfunction and HSA failure tend to co-occur.

### 4.2 Internal impingement and GIRD

Glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD) is defined as a loss of internal rotation in the dominant shoulder of >18–20° versus the non-dominant side. Burkhart's working definition is a >20° loss (https://oliverfinlay.com/assets/pdf/cools%20et%20al%20(2008)%20internal%20impingement%20in%20the%20tennis%20player%20-%20rehabilitation%20guidelines.pdf). Posterior capsule tightness is the proximate cause; a tight pec major and pec minor exacerbate it.

Mechanistically, "tightness of the pectoralis major creates an anterior force on the glenohumeral joint with a consequent decrease in stability," and tight pec minor "limits scapular upward rotation, external rotation, and posterior tilt, thereby reducing subacromial space" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2577490/). HSA-dominant athletes therefore face a paradox: the action that drives racket speed is the same action that, when pec stiffness builds up, contributes to impingement. Eccentric-bias training and active mobility work on the pec group is the standard countermeasure.

### 4.3 Compensation cascades

When HSA timing is mistimed (firing too early or too late relative to trunk rotation), the typical compensations and their downstream injury risks are:

| HSA error | Compensation | Injury risk |
|---|---|---|
| HSA fires too early (arm leads trunk) | Trunk decelerates abruptly to "wait" for arm | Lumbar oblique strain; loss of racket speed |
| HSA fires too late (arm trails trunk) | Wrist and forearm whip to recover speed | Lateral epicondylitis; ulnar collateral ligament stress |
| HSA amplitude is insufficient | Active pronation increases | ECRB overload |
| HSA produced by anterior deltoid not pec | Subacromial impingement pattern | Rotator cuff irritation |

## 5. HSA Across Stroke Types

### 5.1 Serve

In the tennis serve, the dominant velocity contributor is shoulder internal rotation, with peak angular velocities of ~2,420°/s in male professionals (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1451174/full). Internal rotation of the upper arm has been reported to account for **~54.2% of mean linear racket-head velocity at impact** in the serve. HSA contribution is smaller in the serve than in the forehand because the racket arrives at the ball through a more vertical path; the dominant action is humeral IR within the abducted-elevated position.

A useful summary: in the **forehand HSA + ISR jointly drive racket speed (~25% + ~40% = ~65%)**; in the **serve, ISR dominates alone (~54%)** with HSA contributing relatively less because the trunk side-flexion replaces some of the chest-closure that a forehand uses.

### 5.2 Two-handed backhand

The two-handed backhand mirrors the forehand in the non-dominant arm (the lead arm performs HSA from the opposite side). HSA on the dominant arm is replaced by horizontal abduction. Empirically, peak racket head speeds in the 2H backhand are 5–10% below forehand peaks at matched effort.

### 5.3 One-handed backhand

The dominant arm performs **horizontal abduction** rather than HSA, driven by posterior deltoid, infraspinatus, teres minor, and middle trapezius/rhomboids. EMG studies of the open-stance forehand have specifically contrasted lat dorsi and posterior deltoid activation patterns to show this stroke-type difference (https://www.academia.edu/103045319/Does_the_Kinetic_Chain_Work_in_the_Forehand_Drive_Open_Stance_What_Electromyographic_Analysis_of_the_latissimus_Dorsi_and_the_Posterior_Deltoid_Muscles_Showed_Us).

### 5.4 Overhead/smash

Mechanically a serve variant; HSA contribution is similar to the serve.

### 5.5 Volley

HSA is minimal. Volleys are short, contact-control strokes with limited shoulder excursion. Pec major still stabilises the joint, but power generation is dominated by trunk and contact-stable arm position.

### 5.6 Cross-sport reference: baseball pitching and javelin

Throwing biomechanics literature is the richest source for IR/HSA coupling because the arm motion is more isolated than in tennis. Peak IR angular velocities in pitching reach **~6,500°/s near ball release**, with subscapularis as the highest-firing internal rotator, followed by latissimus dorsi and pectoralis major (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9076771/). Tennis can borrow two findings:

1. The IR triad ordering (subscap → lat → pec) is robust across overhand throwing.
2. Late cocking eccentrically loads the IR triad; this is the tennis-equivalent "shoulder-hip separation at peak backswing" loading the pec.

Javelin shows the same coupling but with a longer acceleration phase, reinforcing the principle that long-axis rotation is the terminal velocity step.

## 6. Training — Evidence-Based Routes to Better HSA

### 6.1 Strength: cable and dumbbell variants

Cable crossover (low-to-high or high-to-low) and dumbbell fly are both transverse-plane HSA exercises, but they differ mechanically:

- **Dumbbell fly** loads HSA only at the lengthened end (gravity vector aligns with HSA torque only when the arm is wide). Resistance drops to near-zero at the top of the rep.
- **Cable crossover** maintains tension throughout HSA range because the cable line of action is roughly constant. The cable variant is therefore preferred for tennis because the HSA action in a forehand needs torque across the whole excursion.

The standing cable rotation at chest height — set perpendicular to the cable, hands together as if holding a racket, pull across body — is the closest gym analogue to the on-court forehand HSA pattern (https://www.asics.com/ie/en-ie/tennis-advice/best-strength-training-exercises-for-tennis-player/). It loads HSA + trunk rotation simultaneously, which preserves the kinetic chain coupling.

### 6.2 Power: medicine-ball rotational throws

Medicine-ball rotational shotput throws (1–3 kg ball, perpendicular-to-target stance) produce an HSA pattern at high contractile velocities. Peer-reviewed studies on tennis-specific resistance training repeatedly identify rotational med-ball work as the highest-transfer drill for groundstroke speed.

### 6.3 Eccentric pectoralis training

Given the HSA → impingement risk pathway (§4.2), eccentric-biased pec work is doubly indicated. Slow-eccentric cable fly, with 4–5 s eccentrics and explosive concentrics, trains both the SSC pre-stretch tolerance and the impingement-protective muscle stiffness. The dosing principle from Achilles tendinopathy research transfers — eccentric overload at moderate volumes drives tendon remodeling.

### 6.4 What does NOT transfer well

- Bench press: pectoral development without rotational specificity. Useful as base strength but not transfer-rich.
- Push-up: similar limitation; closed-chain HSA in a fixed sagittal plane.
- Resistance-band internal rotation (sidelying, arm fixed): trains ISR in isolation, divorced from HSA. Useful for cuff health, low transfer to forehand speed.

### 6.5 Mobility prerequisites

Pec major and pec minor stretching (doorway stretch, supine pec major stretch on a foam roller) are cited as standard tennis-shoulder maintenance to prevent the GIRD/impingement cascade. Posterior capsule sleeper stretch is the partner mobility for the same problem (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2577490/).

## 7. Detecting HSA Quality from Video — Kinematic Markers

### 7.1 The shoulder horizontal adduction angle from 2D pose

From standard 2D pose keypoints (MediaPipe Pose, OpenPose, BlazePose), the most stable HSA proxy is the **angle between the line shoulder→elbow and the line shoulder→opposite-shoulder**, projected onto the camera plane.

Concretely, with keypoints `R_SHO`, `L_SHO`, `R_ELB`:

- Vector A = `R_ELB - R_SHO` (upper-arm vector)
- Vector B = `L_SHO - R_SHO` (cross-chest reference vector)
- HSA angle = `arccos((A · B) / (|A| |B|))`

A small HSA angle (arm pointing toward the off shoulder) means full horizontal adduction. A large HSA angle (arm pointing away from chest, ~180°) means full horizontal abduction. Tracking the time series of this angle through the swing gives:

- **HSA excursion** = max angle (peak backswing) − min angle (post-contact follow-through).
- **HSA peak angular velocity** = max(|dθ/dt|).
- **Time-to-peak HSA velocity relative to contact** — measure the frame index of peak velocity and subtract the contact frame index.

MediaPipe shows excellent reliability for shoulder adduction-type measurements: ICC of 0.99 vs goniometer/inclinometer ground truth, and 2D MediaPipe joint angle ranges of motion correlate at r ≈ 0.95 with Qualisys 3D motion capture, with mean coefficient of variation below 10% (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11086111/).

### 7.2 Camera-angle dependency

A frontal (chest-on) view of the player gives the cleanest HSA angle. A side view collapses the HSA plane onto the camera depth axis and produces large errors. The literature is explicit: shoulder abduction-type angles "exhibited a tendency for errors that increased with variations in the camera position and increasing abduction angles" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10416158/). For a tennis-side-view shoot — the standard rear-baseline camera — HSA must be inferred indirectly from the elbow trajectory crossing the chest plane.

### 7.3 The pec-humerus angle as proxy

A robust 2D proxy is the angle formed by `R_HIP → R_SHO → R_ELB`. Closing this angle through the swing means the upper arm is sweeping forward across the chest. This proxy works reasonably from a side view because the gross anteroposterior excursion of the upper arm projects onto that plane.

### 7.4 Common visual error patterns

Translatable to detection rules:

| Error | Visual signature | Numeric rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|
| HSA absent (arm-only swing) | Elbow stays out wide; no chest-cross | HSA excursion < 30° (frontal view) |
| HSA late (arm trails) | Trunk rotates fully but elbow stays back | Trunk rotation peak − HSA peak time > 60 ms |
| HSA with elbow lock-out | Straight-arm slap; no ISR runway | Elbow angle stays > 160° throughout |
| Forearm-substitution pattern | Wrist breaks early, elbow stays high | Wrist angular velocity peak occurs ≥ 50 ms before elbow extension peak |
| Anterior-deltoid only HSA | Visible shoulder-shrug; chest does not close | Trunk angle change < 30° while HSA excursion is normal |

### 7.5 Time-to-peak HSA velocity relative to contact

Empirically, in well-coordinated forehands the peak HSA angular velocity occurs between 50 ms before contact and contact itself; ISR peak occurs slightly after HSA peak; pronation peak occurs at or just after contact. A detection chain that flags violations of this ordering is a high-yield diagnostic for the HSA → ISR → pronation coupling failure.

### 7.6 Practical tracking pipeline (suggested)

1. Run 60 fps video through MediaPipe Pose (3D landmarks if a frontal angle is available; 2D otherwise).
2. Compute HSA-angle time series + elbow angle + wrist angle + trunk yaw (hip-shoulder line).
3. Identify contact frame (ball-impact detection or audio cue).
4. Extract: HSA excursion, HSA peak angular velocity, HSA peak time relative to contact, trunk-yaw peak time, elbow-extension peak time.
5. Rank-order the peaks; flag deviations from the expected proximal-to-distal order.

This pipeline aligns with the existing project infrastructure (60 fps spec, pose-based KPI evaluation, foundation-layer diagnostics).

## 9. Open Questions / Where the Literature Is Thin

1. **Direct EMG of pec major during the tennis forehand at multiple skill levels.** Most pec EMG data come from the serve or pitching; forehand-specific pec EMG with skill-level stratification is sparse.
2. **Quantitative moment-arm decomposition between clavicular and sternocostal heads at the humeral positions specific to forehand contact** (not generic anatomy ranges).
3. **Whether HSA training transfer can be tested in a randomized controlled trial** with racket speed as the outcome — currently most evidence is observational/cross-sectional.
4. **The exact functional border between "HSA failure" and "ISR failure" in lateral-epicondylitis players** — clinically these co-occur but the dominant fault is rarely isolated experimentally.
5. **Sex differences.** Most peer-reviewed forehand kinematic data are male-dominant; the strength/velocity scaling for female players is under-characterized.

These gaps are the obvious follow-up research targets for a tennis biomechanics program.
